## What should you actually look for in a mold inspection company?

The inspector should document moisture sources, not just mold spots, because mold is a symptom and the leak behind the wall is the actual problem. A good report pairs a moisture meter reading at the affected wall with a plain explanation of where the water is coming from, whether that's a failed riser in a Diamond Head co-op or condensation on a cold water line in a Makiki walk-up.

Sampling should be independent when it matters for a transaction. If you're buying a Kakaako condo and the seller's disclosure mentions a prior leak, ask whether air or surface samples go to a third-party lab rather than being read in-house. That keeps the result credible to an AOAO board, an insurer, or a future buyer's attorney.

Ask what happens after a positive result. A company that only inspects has to hand you off; a company that also remediates, like AAA Water Damage Restoration & Carpet Care, can usually schedule the next step on the same visit instead of making you find and vet a second contractor during the peak of a tropical wet season.

## What happens during a mold inspection and testing visit?

A typical visit runs three phases: a visual walkthrough with a moisture meter, targeted sampling where visual inspection alone can't confirm the extent, and a written report with recommendations.

1. **Visual and moisture inspection.** The technician checks the suspect area and any adjoining walls, since moisture travels along studs and through single-wall construction common in older Kaimuki and Moiliili homes.
2. **Sampling, if needed.** Air samples (collected with a calibrated pump and cassette) or surface swabs go to an independent lab for species identification when visual evidence isn't conclusive.
3. **Written report.** You get documented findings, moisture readings, and a scope recommendation, useful for an insurance claim, a real estate transaction, or simply deciding whether remediation is necessary.
