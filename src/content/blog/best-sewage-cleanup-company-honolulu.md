## Should you call a plumber or a restoration company first?

Call a plumber first only if sewage is actively backing up from a clog or a failed line that has not been stopped; call a restoration company once the source is shut off and contaminated water needs to be removed and the structure sanitized.

A plumber's job ends when the pipe is clear or repaired. The water and whatever it touched, carpet, baseboards, cabinet toe-kicks, drywall, is still contaminated and still soaking into the structure. That's a separate scope, and it's why Honolulu homeowners often need both calls within the same hour. The [CDC's guidance on cleaning safely after a disaster](https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/safety/index.html) treats sewage-contaminated water as a category that requires protective equipment and proper disposal of anything porous it touched, not a mop-and-bucket job.

## What does a proper sewage cleanup actually include?

A proper sewage cleanup removes the contaminated water, discards porous materials the water soaked into, disinfects every affected surface, and dries the structure to a verified moisture reading, not just a visually dry one.

The typical sequence:

1. **Extraction** of standing contaminated water with pumps or truck-mounted equipment.
2. **Removal** of carpet, pad, baseboards, and any drywall below the flood line that touched the water, since these can't be reliably sanitized.
3. **Disinfection** of remaining hard surfaces with an EPA-registered antimicrobial.
4. **Structural drying** with air movers and dehumidifiers, monitored with a moisture meter until readings return to a dry standard, not just until the floor feels dry to the touch.
5. **Odor control**, since sewage odor that lingers after drying usually means something porous got missed.

Skipping step 2 is the most common shortcut, and the one most likely to cause a mold problem two to three weeks later. ## Questions to ask (and red flags to avoid) before hiring a sewage cleanup crew

Ask whether the crew plans to remove porous materials that contacted sewage, how they confirm the structure is dry before closing the job, and whether they document the loss for your insurance claim; a crew that plans to just clean and dry carpet pad or drywall that touched Category 3 water is cutting a corner that often resurfaces as mold or odor.

Questions worth asking before you sign anything:

- Will you remove and discard porous materials that contacted the sewage, or just clean them in place?
- How do you confirm the area is dry: a moisture meter reading, or a visual check?
- Will you provide daily drying logs and photos for my insurance claim?
- What's included in the written scope before work starts?

If a contractor's license status matters to you for any work beyond the cleanup itself, Hawaii's [DCCA Business Check](https://cca.hawaii.gov/business-check/) lets you verify a license before you sign anything.
