## What to Do in the First 30 Minutes

The first 30 minutes decide how much of your inventory and paperwork survives. Stop the source if you can do it safely: close the nearest shutoff valve, or if it's a sprinkler head, don't touch the riser, that's a job for a fire protection contractor, not you.

Cut power to any outlet or panel the water has reached, from the breaker, never by reaching into a wet area yourself. Move inventory, paper files, and electronics off the floor and away from the wet zone. Photograph the source and the standing water before you move anything. For a fuller walkthrough of the first day, see our guide on [what to do in the first 24 hours after water damage](/blog/what-to-do-first-24-hours-water-damage/).

## Office Water Damage: Who to Call and in What Order

In a leased office, call your building engineer or property manager first if the water is coming from a shared line, the ceiling, or a neighboring suite, then the restoration crew, then your insurer. The order matters because extraction only works once the source is actually stopped.

1. **Building engineer or property manager**: isolates the source faster than anyone outside the building, logs the incident for the building's own policy, and usually has an on-call plumber for riser or fire-line issues beyond your suite.
2. **Restoration company**: handles extraction, drying, and moisture mapping for your suite's finishes and contents as part of a full [commercial restoration](/services/commercial-restoration/) process, separate from the building shell, which is typically the landlord's responsibility.
3. **Your insurer or agent**: brought in once the source is controlled and extraction is underway. Having moisture logs ready from the start speeds up the adjuster conversation considerably.

## Fire Sprinkler Discharge: Who Resets It, Who Dries It

A discharged fire sprinkler head needs two separate calls: a fire protection contractor to reset or replace the head and refill the system, and a restoration crew to extract the water and dry the space. A restoration company does not repair or reset sprinkler systems or plumbing lines, that step always belongs to a fire protection contractor or plumber.

Once the fire protection contractor confirms the system is safe and flow has stopped, extraction can start. Sprinkler water is typically clean water at the moment of discharge, but in an occupied commercial space with ceiling tile, insulation, and aged piping, it degrades fast if drying doesn't start within hours. Most commercial drying follows the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard for professional water damage restoration](https://iicrc.org/s500/), which is why moisture readings, not guesswork, decide when a space is actually dry.

## Restaurant and Retail: Reopening Fast Without Cutting Corners

Restaurants and retail businesses need the health department and the restoration crew working in parallel, not in sequence, because every closed day costs revenue. Water in a commercial kitchen usually means pulling wet flooring near prep areas, drying under equipment lines, and documenting food inventory loss for both the insurer and the inspector.

Call your health department contact if the water reached food storage or prep surfaces; reopening needs their sign-off separate from the drying work. Retail spaces split the same way: structural drying handled by the restoration crew, inventory loss documented for the claim, and the reopening decision made by you. ## Condo and AOAO Common Areas: Who Coordinates the Claim

When water damage hits a condo building's common area, the AOAO's property manager or resident manager calls the restoration company and coordinates with the building's master insurance policy, while individual owners handle their own unit policy for interior finishes.

This split matters in Waikiki, Kakaako, and Ala Moana towers, where a single failed riser on an upper floor often damages a common hallway, the unit directly below, and sometimes several units down the stack. The resident manager's incident report becomes the reference point both insurers use, so getting the restoration crew's moisture readings into that report early keeps the claim from stalling over whose policy pays for what.

## What to Document for the Commercial Insurance Claim

Photograph the source and the standing water before anything is moved, then keep a running list of damaged inventory, equipment, and fixtures with approximate replacement cost.

- Photos or video of the source and affected areas before cleanup starts
- A list of damaged inventory, furniture, and equipment, with serial numbers or receipts where you have them
- The restoration crew's moisture logs and drying report, which document that mitigation started promptly
- Any incident report from the building engineer, property manager, or AOAO
- Receipts for emergency mitigation steps you paid for out of pocket

The Insurance Information Institute notes that documenting a loss thoroughly and promptly is one of the biggest factors in how smoothly a commercial claim settles.
