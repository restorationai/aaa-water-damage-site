## How much does commercial water damage restoration cost in Honolulu?

Most commercial water losses in Honolulu fall between $3,000 for a small, contained clean-water event and well over $100,000 for a multi-floor loss in a high-rise. Where a specific job lands depends on square footage, water category, and how many days of drying equipment the space needs.

## What actually drives a commercial bill up or down?

Five variables move a commercial water loss estimate more than anything else: total square footage, water category, how long the water sat, drying equipment days, and whether flooring gets cleaned in place or replaced.

**Square footage and number of floors or units.** A single office suite is a different job than a full floor of a Waikiki hotel or a Kakaako condo tower where water travels down through multiple units. More affected square footage means more extraction time, more air movers, and more dehumidifiers running simultaneously.

**Water category.** Category 1 (clean) water from a burst supply line costs less to remediate than Category 2 (gray) water from an appliance or Category 3 (black) water from a sewer backup or floor-drain overflow, per the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). Category 3 losses require more porous material removal (carpet pad, baseboards, sometimes drywall) and antimicrobial treatment, which adds labor and disposal cost.

**How long the water sat before extraction started.** Honolulu's humidity means a commercial space with soaked carpet and drywall can show visible mold growth within 24 to 48 hours, year-round. A loss discovered Monday morning that happened Friday night after close costs more to remediate than one caught within hours, because more material has absorbed water and mold risk goes up.

**Drying equipment days.** Commercial jobs often run larger LGR dehumidifiers and more air movers than a residential job, and they may need to run after-hours or overnight to avoid disrupting business operations the next day. Extra equipment and after-hours labor both add to the final invoice.

**Carpet tile and flooring: clean in place or replace?** Commercial carpet tile that was only exposed to Category 1 water for a short time can often be extracted and dried in place, which costs far less than replacement. Category 2 or 3 water, or carpet that stayed wet more than 48 hours, usually gets pulled and replaced instead, since cleaning can't fully address contamination or the padding underneath.

## Why does faster mitigation lower the total claim?

Every day a commercial space stays wet adds cost: more materials absorb water, mold risk climbs, and the business loses operating days. Getting extraction and drying started within hours, not days, is the single biggest lever for keeping the total bill, and the business interruption, as low as possible.

A restaurant that can't open for a week loses revenue every one of those days, on top of the restoration bill. A property manager with an empty retail suite loses rent. Commercial property policies sometimes include business interruption or loss-of-income coverage, which can offset some of that, but it depends entirely on the policy language. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) notes that documenting a loss promptly and keeping receipts helps commercial claims move faster, the same principle that applies to residential losses.

## How does commercial insurance usually cover a water loss?

Commercial water damage coverage usually depends on the source of the water and whether a building has a master policy (common in condo and mixed-use towers) layered with individual tenant or unit-owner coverage. Check the specific policy; this is general information, not coverage advice.

In a single-tenant commercial building, the building owner's commercial property policy typically responds to sudden, accidental water damage, similar to a homeowners policy: a burst pipe or failed water heater is usually covered, while a slow, long-term leak from poor maintenance usually isn't. Flood water from outside the building (storm surge, overflowing streams, surface water) is not covered by a standard commercial property policy and needs separate flood coverage.

In a condo tower or mixed-use building, the AOAO or building association's master policy often covers common-area damage and sometimes the building's own structure, while each tenant or unit owner's policy covers their own contents and improvements. When a loss starts in one unit and travels into others, as it often does in Waikiki and Kakaako high-rises, the master policy and multiple tenant policies can all end up involved, which is why documentation from the first hour matters. A commercial restoration crew that photographs moisture readings floor by floor and keeps a clear drying log gives every adjuster involved the same facts to work from.

Sewer or drain backups and sump pump failures are typically excluded from standard commercial property coverage unless the policy includes a water backup endorsement, so don't assume that coverage exists without checking.

## What should a commercial mitigation estimate actually include?

A commercial mitigation estimate should break out extraction, equipment-day counts, material removal, antimicrobial treatment if needed, and a drying monitoring schedule, not just one lump-sum number.

Look for line items covering: initial water extraction (by square footage or labor hours), the number of air movers and dehumidifiers and how many days they'll run, any carpet, pad, or drywall removal with disposal, antimicrobial application for Category 2 or 3 water, and a daily moisture-monitoring visit until the space reads dry per IICRC guidelines. A vague one-line estimate ("water mitigation, $X") makes it hard for an adjuster, or you, to verify the work matches the bill.
