## What does a commercial water loss need that a house doesn't?

A commercial water loss needs after-hours mobilization, coordination with tenants or guests, and a drying plan built around keeping the business open, not just drying one room and leaving. A residential crew that shows up for a single-family house is rarely set up for any of that.

Downtime is the real cost in a commercial loss. A flooded retail floor or a soaked hotel corridor isn't just water damage, it's lost revenue for every hour the space is unusable. A company equipped for commercial work schedules drying equipment placement around your operating hours when possible, phases the work so part of the space can reopen while another section dries, and keeps a property manager or ownership group updated daily instead of going dark until the job is done.

Tenant and guest coordination matters just as much. In a mixed-use building or a hotel, the crew needs to communicate what's happening, how long equipment will be running, and when common areas are safe to use again, usually through building management rather than directly to every resident.

## What should a commercial crew actually handle on an office, retail, restaurant or hotel loss?

The scope depends on the property type: offices need fast extraction around cubicles, server rooms and flooring systems; restaurants need extraction and drying around commercial kitchen equipment and grease-trap areas; hotels need overnight turnaround so rooms stay sellable.

Office losses on Oahu often involve raised flooring, carpet tile, and electronics that can't get wet twice, so extraction speed in the first few hours matters more than almost anything else. Retail losses usually center on flooring and merchandise storage, where standing water under shelving or display fixtures gets missed if the crew isn't looking for it.

Restaurant and hotel losses add a layer most residential jobs never see: a commercial kitchen that can't reopen with wet subflooring, or guest rooms that need to be back in inventory before the next check-in. ## How is a high-rise or AOAO condo loss different from a single unit?

A loss in a high-rise or AOAO building almost always touches both common areas and private units, and figuring out which insurance policy pays for which part is the first thing that has to get sorted out. The AOAO's master policy typically covers building structure and common areas; each owner's HO-6 or condo policy usually covers their unit's interior finishes and contents, though governing documents vary by building.

In Honolulu's high-rise towers in Waikiki, Kakaako and downtown, a failed supply line on one floor can soak ceilings, hallway carpet and multiple units below before anyone catches it. That means the restoration company needs to document damage unit by unit and in shared corridors separately, because the AOAO board, the property manager and individual owners will each be filing with different insurers. A crew that treats the whole building as one undifferentiated job creates a documentation headache later.

Good coordination here means one point of contact who keeps the board, the property manager and any affected owners on the same page about what's drying where and why.

## What documentation protects a commercial insurance claim?

Moisture logs, dated photos, and daily drying readings are what separate a claim that gets approved quickly from one that gets questioned. Commercial adjusters expect to see the extent of the loss and the drying progress, not just a final invoice.

At minimum, ask whether the company documents moisture readings at the start of the job and at each visit until materials reach a dry standard, photographs the affected areas before equipment goes in, and keeps a record of when equipment was placed and removed. That record is what supports a claim if the insurer asks why drywall was removed or why drying took a certain number of days.

The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) notes that thorough documentation and prompt mitigation are two of the biggest factors in how smoothly a disaster claim gets settled, and that holds just as true for a commercial property as a home. Drying should also follow the structural drying benchmarks laid out in the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry reference for how water losses are supposed to be dried and verified, regardless of which company does the work.

## What should you ask before you sign a commercial work authorization?

Before signing, ask how fast they can mobilize after hours, how they document the loss for your insurer, how they'll communicate with tenants or guests, and whether the scope and equipment plan are put in writing before work starts.

A short list worth going through with any company you're considering:

- Can you respond after hours and on weekends, and what does that actually mean for arrival time?
- Will you provide a written scope of work and equipment list before drying begins?
- How do you document the loss (photos, moisture logs, daily readings) for a commercial claim?
- Who is my single point of contact for updates during the job?
- How do you handle common areas versus individual units or tenant spaces?
- What happens if the source of the water (a pipe, appliance or sprinkler head) still needs a separate contractor to repair it?

If a company can't answer these clearly before you sign, that's worth noting before, not after, equipment is running in your building.
